# Unit 3 — What's Trending?

## Lesson 1 — Street Entertainers

### Vocabulary
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: vocabulary -->

**Street Entertainers**
Read the definitions to learn vocabulary related to street performances:
- **performing:** doing something entertaining for an audience (verb)
- **puppet:** a small model character that you move with your hands (noun)
- **trick:** an entertaining act that looks like magic (noun)
- **controlling:** making something do what you want (verb)
- **floating:** moving in the air (verb)
- **dangerous:** not safe; likely to cause pain or harm (adjective)
- **blow:** to push out air from your mouth (verb)
- **fire:** flames and heat from something burning (noun)

### Grammar
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: grammar -->
<!-- grammar_focus: indefinite_pronouns -->

**Indefinite Pronouns**
We use indefinite pronouns to talk about people, places, or things without saying exactly who, where, or what they are.
- **Places:** *Everywhere* means all places. *Somewhere* means an unspecified place.
- **People:** *Nobody* means no people. *Someone* means an unknown person. *Everybody* means all people.
- **Things:** *Everything* means all things. *Something* means an unspecified thing.
- **Negative sentences/Questions:** If the verb or adverb is negative, use *anyone*, *anything*, or *anywhere*.

*Example:* "Everywhere I go, people always ask me what my trick is, but I never tell anyone the secret of my act. Nobody knows."

### Practice
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: practice -->

1. Read the paragraph and choose the correct option:
   Before he became known to 1 (someone / everyone / anyone) as James Bond, the actor Pierce Brosnan worked as a street entertainer in London. He was training to become an artist, but he met 2 (somewhere / someone / something) who showed him the art of fire-eating. Soon after Brosnan started performing, 3 (nobody / everybody / somebody) who worked as a circus agent saw his act and thought it was amazing. Brosnan performed his fire-eating trick on the TV show Muppets Tonight. 4 (Nobody / Everyone / Everything) knows exactly how it happened, but there was a problem with the fire-eating trick and Pierce got serious burns. He didn't let 5 (anyone / everybody / nobody) know he was hurt until the end of the show.

### Teacher Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: pedagogy -->

**Reading (Optional):** Explain what street entertainers do. More popular street entertainment includes: mime, acrobatics, juggling, contortion, gymnastics, and comedy routines.
**Speaking - Confident Communicator:** When students need to talk for a minute or more, tell them to make notes about what they want to say before starting. They should use key words, symbols, or images instead of writing full sentences, and use fillers like *umm, well,* and *as I was saying* to give them thinking time.
**Grammar Extra Practice:** Write more examples on the board to get students started with indefinite pronouns. For example: *Everyone in my family likes going to concerts. Nobody I know spends much time playing video games.*

### Cultural Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: cultural_context -->

When people play music in the street and ask for donations, it is called "busking". People can busk alone or with a band. They use almost any instrument you can think of, including violins, accordions, cellos, guitars, and so on. It is a very common practice in many European and North American cities.

## Lesson 2 — The Music Industry

### Vocabulary
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: vocabulary -->

**The Music Industry**
Words to describe jobs in the music industry. This person...
- **producer:** records the songs in a music studio.
- **lead vocalist:** is the main singer in a band.
- **songwriter:** writes songs for a band.
- **backup singer:** sings in the background.
- **stylist:** focuses on the band's clothes, hair, and makeup.
- **sound technician:** checks equipment so the audience can hear the band.
- **manager:** controls the band's business side.
- **director:** makes music videos.

### Grammar
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: grammar -->
<!-- grammar_focus: used_to -->

**Used to**
- *Used to* is used for a past action or habit that is NOT happening now.
- After *used to*, use the base form of the verb.
- In questions and negative statements, use *did/didn't* + *use to*.

*Examples:* 
- Bands **used to write** their own music. (Affirmative)
- People **didn't use to do** that. (Negative)
- **Did** you **use to be** in a band? (Question)

### Practice
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: practice -->

1. Complete the sentences with *used to*, *didn't use to*, or *did... use to*:
   a. Where 1 _____ you _____ take music lessons?
   b. I 2 _____ download music, but now I download music every week.
   c. 3 _____ you _____ watch cartoons when you were a kid?
   d. Those guys 4 _____ be in a band called Kings and Queens.
   e. My brother 5 _____ like hip hop, but he does now.

### Teacher Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: pedagogy -->

**Vocabulary Extra Practice:** Ask students to brainstorm music genres and discuss the vocabulary words in relation to different genres (e.g., pop, rock, classical, heavy metal, hip-hop, electronic, blues, jazz, reggae). 
**Pronunciation - reduced form of "used to":** Explain to students that the schwa (/ə/) is the most common sound in English and it represents weak sounds. Have them practice /ju:stə/ in isolation and then identify it in speech.
**Grammar Note:** Let students know that they can contrast their past and present habits by using the word *but*. (e.g., *I didn't use to like classical music, but I enjoy listening to it now*).

### Cultural Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: cultural_context -->

Music videos were widely viewed starting in the 1980s when Music Television (MTV) came on the air as a cable TV channel dedicated to music. Music videos had already existed as a way for bands to promote themselves anywhere in the world when they were on tour. These days, we mostly access music videos online (e.g., YouTube).

## Lesson 3 — Viewing Habits

### Vocabulary
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: vocabulary -->

**TV Viewing**
Terms to describe how we watch TV and videos:
- **binge-watch:** to watch several shows in a TV series at one time (verb).
- **channel-surf:** to go quickly from one channel to another looking for something to watch (verb).
- **commercials:** advertisements on TV (noun plural).
- **episode:** a TV program that is one part of a set (noun).
- **on demand:** ready to watch at any time (adjective).
- **stream:** to play a video or game using wi-fi without downloading it (verb).
- **TV series:** a set of TV shows with the same characters (noun).

### Grammar
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: grammar -->
<!-- grammar_focus: qualifying_phrases -->

**Making Contrasts and Qualifying Phrases**
- To make contrasts, you can use linking words like **but**, **although**, and **though**. 
  *Example:* I rarely watch TV these days, **although** there are some shows I like.
- To make what you're saying more interesting, use qualifying phrases with adjectives: **sort of / kind of** (for moderation) and **really / pretty** (for emphasis).
  *Example:* My favorite show is a science fiction series. It's **kind of** scary and **really** funny.

### Practice
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: practice -->

1. Complete the sentences with words from the TV Viewing vocabulary:
   a. This show is not _____. You can only watch it on Saturday at 6 p.m.
   b. When I don't know what to watch on TV, I _____ until I find something I like.
   c. My favorite _____ is at 8 p.m. every Tuesday night.
   d. Did you watch last night's _____ of City Hospital? It was amazing!
   e. On rainy evenings, I like to stay home and _____ my favorite show.
   f. There is no wi-fi here, so I can't _____ that video on my laptop.
   g. When I watch TV, I often get a snack during the _____.

### Teacher Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: pedagogy -->

**Vocabulary Activity:** As an introduction, ask students what they think the most popular TV shows in their country are now. Ask for a quick show of hands to survey the class to determine the most popular TV shows among them.
**Make it Real:** Saying "I'm really into..." expresses what you like. To refer to the programs someone is watching or binge-watching, teach the phrase: *What TV shows are you into at the moment?*
**Confident Communicator:** Tell students that using qualifying phrases (like *sort of, pretty*) with adjectives is a useful tool for sounding natural when speaking, but they are informal and should generally be avoided in academic writing.

### Cultural Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: cultural_context -->

Entertainment viewing habits have drastically changed over the last two decades. While previous generations gathered around a single TV to watch live broadcasts with commercial breaks, modern audiences—especially younger generations—heavily rely on streaming services, watching shows "on demand," and "binge-watching" entire seasons in one sitting.
