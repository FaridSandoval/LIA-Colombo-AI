# Unit 4 — Your ID

## Lesson 1 — Personality Types

### Vocabulary
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: vocabulary -->

**Personality Types**
Read the definitions to learn words that describe people's personalities:
- **shy:** quiet; nervous and embarrassed.
- **easygoing:** very relaxed; calm.
- **depressed:** sad; very unhappy.
- **lazy:** not wanting to work.
- **optimistic:** positive; thinking good things will happen.
- **impatient:** annoyed because something isn't happening quickly.

### Grammar
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: grammar -->
<!-- grammar_focus: not_as_as -->

**(not) as ... as**
We use **as + adjective + as** to show that two things or people are equal in a particular way. 
We use **not as + adjective + as** to show that they are not equal (one is less than the other).

*Examples:*
- Type Cs get **as stressed as** Type As at work. (They are equally stressed).
- Type Ds **aren't as optimistic as** Type Bs. (Type Bs are more optimistic).
- I'm **not as hard-working as** Danni.

### Practice
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: practice -->

1. Choose the correct option to complete the paragraph:
   Because my sister and I are twins, people think we have the same personality, but we are very different. My sister, Danni, is more of a Type A personality. I'm more of a Type B. Danni 1 (isn't as patient as / is as patient as) I am. She gets mad about small things, but I don't. In general, Type A people 2 (aren't as easygoing as / are as easygoing as) Type B people. We both want to buy our things and leave quickly, so I'm 3 (as impatient as / not as impatient as) Danni when I have to wait in line. I think we are both outgoing, and I'm 4 (as friendly as / not as friendly as) her. But I'm 5 (as hard-working as / not as hard-working as) Danni, so I go out more often!

### Teacher Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: pedagogy -->

**Vocabulary:** Brainstorm adjectives that students may need to talk about their friends' personalities and write them on the board (e.g., *happy, sad, energetic, lazy, clever, friendly, talkative, hardworking, funny, reserved*). Have students categorize the words into negative and positive meanings.
**Grammar:** Give students an example from your life and use comparatives to talk about how you and a friend are different. Have them compare people they know (e.g., brother/sister, Dad/Mom) using the target grammar.
**Confident Communicator:** Teach students expressions to estimate when talking about personality types: *I think I'm roughly 25% Type A... / Off the top of my head, I'd say I'm about 50% Type D...*

### Answer Key
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: answer_key -->

**Practice ((not) as ... as):**
1. isn't as patient as
2. aren't as easygoing as
3. as impatient as
4. as friendly as
5. not as hard-working as

### Cultural Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: cultural_context -->

Personality theories like Type A, B, C, and D are popular in Western psychology and corporate culture to describe behavior patterns, especially regarding stress and teamwork. For example, "Type A" is culturally understood in English as someone highly competitive, organized, and ambitious.

## Lesson 2 — Teamwork and Personality

### Vocabulary
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: vocabulary -->

**Different Personalities in Teamwork**
Words to describe the roles people take in groups. This person...
- **leader:** is responsible for a group of people.
- **expert:** has specialized knowledge.
- **loner:** likes to be alone.
- **workaholic:** likes to work a lot.
- **creative thinker:** has new and imaginative ideas.
- **risk taker:** likes to try new and sometimes dangerous things.

### Grammar
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: grammar -->
<!-- grammar_focus: superlative_adjectives -->

**Superlative Adjectives**
We use superlative adjectives to compare more than two things.
- Use **the** before a superlative adjective.
- For adjectives that are one syllable, add **-est** (e.g., *tall -> the tallest*).
- For adjectives that are two syllables and end with -y, change the 'y' to 'i' and add **-est** (e.g., *happy -> the happiest*).
- For adjectives that are two or more syllables, use **the most** before the adjective (e.g., *creative -> the most creative*).
- Irregular forms: **good -> the best**, **bad -> the worst**.

### Practice
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: practice -->

1. Complete the sentences with the correct superlative form of the adjective in parentheses:
   a. Diana is __________ (creative) person I know.
   b. The day I won this award was __________ (happy) day of my life.
   c. Fernando is __________ (tall) student in this class.
   d. Esmeralda is __________ (talkative) student in this class.
   e. The Green Garden is __________ (good) restaurant in town.

### Teacher Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: pedagogy -->

**Vocabulary:** Ask students to write an example sentence for each type of person about someone they know. Connecting new words to their own lives helps with memorization (e.g., *My uncle Fred is a natural leader.*).
**Grammar Additional Activity:** Ask students to name jobs and write them on the board. Tell them to describe the best personality for each job using superlatives (e.g., *I think police officers should be the most patient people*). 
**Make it Real:** In spoken English, people often use the superlative to show that something is *really* good or bad, and they emphasize the word "the" by pronouncing it /ði:/ (e.g., *That was THE most amazing meal!*). This is an example of hyperbole.

### Answer Key
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: answer_key -->

**Practice (Superlative Adjectives):**
a. the most creative
b. the happiest
c. the tallest
d. the most talkative
e. the best

### Cultural Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: cultural_context -->

In modern workplaces and academic environments, teamwork is highly valued. Concepts like the "creative thinker" or the "expert" are often discussed in team-building activities to ensure a balanced group dynamic where all personality types can contribute effectively.

## Lesson 3 — Who Are You Like?

### Vocabulary
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: vocabulary -->

**Characteristics**
More adjectives to describe someone's character. Someone who is...
- **bossy:** tells people what to do.
- **smart:** is intelligent.
- **rude:** isn't polite.
- **generous:** enjoys giving things to people.
- **adventurous:** loves new and exciting experiences.
- **selfish:** thinks only about himself or herself.
- **honest:** tells the truth.

### Grammar
<!-- audience: student -->
<!-- topic: grammar -->
<!-- grammar_focus: describing_past_habits -->

*(Note: In this lesson the focus shifts towards Listening/Speaking and categorizing characteristics).*

**Describing Changes in Personality**
Use these phrases to talk about how your personality has changed as you grew up:
- *When I was younger / in kindergarten / in high school, I was talkative.*
- *I used to be a little impatient, but I'm more relaxed now.*
- *Until I went to high school / university, I was the shiest person in the class.*

### Teacher Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: pedagogy -->

**Vocabulary:** For students to become independent learners, encourage them to record vocabulary using different methods (lists in a notebook, index cards, notes on a smartphone). 
**Listening Skill - Predict:** To predict is to figure out or anticipate what you think will happen. In predicting, past experiences are important. Have students predict if people become more similar to their parents as they get older based on their own families.
**Speaking:** Play a game of "Guess Who". Think of a well-known character from a movie or TV series. Describe their positive and negative characteristics and ask your partner to guess who it is.
**Cultural/Language Note:** Explain the difference between *fraternal* and *identical* twins. Also, write the terms *to look like* (appearance), *to like* (preferences), and *to be like* (personality) on the board and elicit the differences.

### Answer Key
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: answer_key -->

**Practice (Characteristics):**
- **Positive characteristics:** smart, generous, adventurous, honest.
- **Negative characteristics:** bossy, rude, selfish.

### Cultural Notes
<!-- audience: teacher_only -->
<!-- topic: cultural_context -->

When discussing characteristics, "bossy" is often perceived negatively in English-speaking cultures because independence and equality are highly valued. A more positive spin on this trait in a professional setting might be calling someone a "strong leader" or "assertive."
